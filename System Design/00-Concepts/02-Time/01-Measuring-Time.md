## The Fundamental Idea of Time Measurement

Time cannot be directly observed.

What we actually measure is:

> A repeating physical process.

Every physical clock works by:

1. Finding something that repeats.
2. Counting how many times it repeats.
3. Mapping that count to seconds.

This repeating process is called **oscillation**.

---

### Vibration / Cycle

A vibration (or cycle) is:

> One complete back-and-forth motion around a center point.

Example:

Center → Right → Center → Left → Center

That full motion = 1 cycle = 1 vibration.

---

### Frequency

Frequency tells:

> How many cycles happen in 1 second.

Unit: Hertz (Hz)

If something vibrates:

- 1 time per second → 1 Hz
    
- 10 times per second → 10 Hz
    

---

### Time Period

Time Period tells:

> How long one cycle takes.

It is the inverse of frequency.

T = 1 / f

If frequency = 5 Hz  
Then period = 0.2 seconds

## 4️⃣ Quartz Crystal Clocks

Now we reach modern clocks.

### Piezoelectric Effect

Quartz has a property:

> When voltage is applied, it physically vibrates.

This vibration is:

- Extremely fast
    
- Extremely stable
    
- Very predictable
    

---

### Why 32,768 Hz?

32,768 = 2^15

This number was chosen deliberately.

Reason:

Digital circuits use binary counters. 
Dividing by 2 is extremely easy in hardware.

If crystal vibrates at 32,768 Hz:

You divide by 2 fifteen times:

32768 → 16384 → 8192 → ... → 1

After 15 divisions, you get 1 pulse per second.

This makes clock circuits:

- Cheap
- Simple 
- Low power

Important:

Quartz does NOT naturally prefer 32,768 Hz.

Engineers cut and shape the crystal so that its natural vibration is near that frequency.

---

### Is Quartz Perfect?

No.

Even if designed for 32,768 Hz:

Actual vibration might be:

- 32,768.02 Hz
- 32,767.98 Hz

Tiny difference.

But tiny difference × millions of cycles = drift.

---

### Sources of Drift in Quartz

1. **Manufacturing variation** 
	No two quartz crystals are perfectly identical. Even though they are designed to vibrate at the same nominal frequency (for example, 32,768 Hz), microscopic differences in how each crystal is cut, shaped, and mounted result in slight frequency variations. These differences may be extremely small, but over time they accumulate. As a result, two clocks built with different quartz crystals—even if placed side by side under identical conditions—will slowly drift apart and show slightly different times after a long enough period.
2. **Temperature** 
	Quartz crystals are most stable around room temperature (approximately 25°C). When the temperature increases or decreases, the crystal slightly expands or contracts, which changes its physical dimensions and elastic properties. Since the oscillation frequency depends on these properties, even small temperature variations can cause the frequency to either increase or decrease slightly. In a server rack, for example, heat generated by CPUs can raise the ambient temperature, and this thermal variation can affect the quartz oscillator inside the system’s clock, leading to small but measurable time drift over time.
    
**3. Voltage variation** 
	The quartz oscillator relies on a stable electrical signal to maintain consistent vibration. If the supply voltage fluctuates—such as when a battery level drops or power delivery becomes unstable—the amplitude and stability of the oscillation can be affected. Although modern circuits regulate voltage to reduce this effect, small variations can still introduce minor frequency shifts, contributing to time drift.

**4. Mechanical stress** 
	Physical shocks(Dropping your phone), vibrations, or continuous mechanical stress can slightly deform the crystal or alter its mounting tension. Since the oscillation frequency depends on the crystal’s precise shape and mechanical properties, even minor structural changes can cause small frequency deviations. For example, dropping a device or exposing it to repeated vibration may lead to subtle but measurable changes in clock accuracy over time.

Even a very small frequency error accumulates over time.

If a clock runs slightly fast or slow, the tiny error compounds every second.

Two clocks will slowly diverge over time.

This phenomenon is called **Clock Drift**.

Clock drift is measured in **PPM (parts per million)**.

1 ppm means:

> The clock drifts by 1 microsecond per second.

So in every second, the clock is off by 1 microsecond compared to a perfect reference.

Now calculate accumulation:

There are 86,400 seconds in one day.

Drift per day ≈ 86,400 microseconds  
≈ 86 milliseconds per day.

There are approximately 31.5 million seconds in one year.

Drift per year ≈ 31.5 seconds per year.

So if two computers both drift at around 1 ppm in opposite directions and are not synchronized for several years, their clocks could differ by minutes.

Even smaller ppm values accumulate significantly over long periods.

This is why unsynchronized clocks in distributed systems can cause:

- Incorrect event ordering
- Invalid timestamps
- Expired tokens being accepted
- Logs appearing out of order
- Data inconsistencies

Real quartz drift is often 10–50 ppm in cheaper systems.

So real-world drift can be:

- Several seconds per month
- Or minutes per year without synchronization


##  Atomic Clocks 

Instead of measuring time using a mechanical vibration (like a pendulum) or a crystal oscillation (like quartz), atomic clocks measure time using a **fundamental atomic property** — the natural energy transition frequency of atoms such as cesium-133.

These atomic transitions are governed by the laws of quantum physics. They are not dependent on:
- Mechanical motion
- Material expansion or contraction
- Manufacturing precision
- External voltage stability

Hence an Atomic clock differs less than one second in 100 million years.

> **TAI Scale** -> Weighted average of 400 clocks worldwide.

## Greenwich Mean Time (GMT)

GMT originally referred to:

> The mean solar time at the Prime Meridian (0° longitude) in Greenwich, London.

Historically:

- Time was based on Earth's rotation.
- Noon was when the Sun was highest in the sky at Greenwich.
- Other countries defined their time relative to GMT.

So GMT was an **astronomical time standard**.

It slows down slightly. 
It fluctuates. 
It is not precise enough for modern systems.

## Coordinated Universal Time (UTC)

UTC is derived from **International Atomic Time (TAI)**, which is based purely on atomic clocks. However, unlike TAI, UTC is periodically adjusted using **leap seconds** to keep it aligned with Earth’s rotational time (solar time GMT). This ensures that civil time does not drift too far from the actual position of the Sun in the sky.

#### Leap Second
Because Earth’s rotation is not perfectly constant, solar time (based on Earth’s rotation) slowly drifts relative to atomic time (TAI). Atomic clocks are extremely stable, while Earth’s rotation slightly slows down and fluctuates due to gravitational effects, tidal forces, and internal dynamics.

To prevent civil time (UTC) from drifting too far from solar time, a **leap second** is occasionally added to UTC. This adjustment keeps UTC within approximately ±0.9 seconds of Earth’s rotational time.

Leap second is generally added in June 31st and Dec 31st.Ignoring this can lead to [Leap Second Crash]((https://sumedhakhatter.medium.com/the-leap-second-crash-312f1d486c65).
