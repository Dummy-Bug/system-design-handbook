# Section 0 — Pre-Band Anchors (must be reflex BEFORE attempting any band)

Even though these don't appear as standalone problems, they are SUB-OPERATIONS inside thousands of problems. If any of these takes more than 5 seconds, that's the floor to fix first.

## 0.1 Range counting (your stated pain point)

> **Now a drill topic:** these anchors are promoted into the topics layer at
> `topics/00-ranges-and-indices/` (range counting, multiples-in-range, complement
> of a contiguous selection, circular-window indexing). Drill them there; this
> section is the analysis-layer reference.

| Range notation | Number of integers in range | Example |
|---|---|---|
| `[a, b]` (closed-closed) | `b - a + 1` | `[3, 7]` → 5 numbers (3,4,5,6,7) |
| `(a, b]` (open-closed) | `b - a` | `(3, 7]` → 4 numbers (4,5,6,7) |
| `[a, b)` (closed-open) | `b - a` | `[3, 7)` → 4 numbers (3,4,5,6) |
| `(a, b)` (open-open) | `b - a - 1` | `(3, 7)` → 3 numbers (4,5,6) |

**Mnemonic:** "Closed adds 1 per closed endpoint, open subtracts 1 per open endpoint, baseline is `b-a`."
- `[a,b]` = `b - a` + 1 (two closed) → +1 total
- `(a,b]` or `[a,b)` = `b - a` (one each) → 0 adjustment
- `(a,b)` = `b - a` - 1 (two open) → -1

**Drill:** answer in 2 seconds for each:
- Numbers in [10, 50]? (41)
- Numbers in (10, 50]? (40)
- Numbers in [10, 50)? (40)
- Numbers in (10, 50)? (39)
- Multiples of 3 in [1, 100]? (33 — since floor(100/3) = 33)
- Multiples of 7 in [50, 200]? (floor(200/7) - floor(49/7) = 28 - 7 = 21)

**LC problems that need this:** *Count Odd Numbers in an Interval Range* (LC 1523, 1209), *Count Commas in Range* (LC 3870, 1149), almost any range-query problem.

## 0.2 Multiples of d in [L, R]

**Fact:** Multiples of d in `[L, R]` = `floor(R/d) - floor((L-1)/d)`.

**Why minus L-1, not L:** the formula `floor(R/d)` counts multiples in `[1, R]`. Subtracting `floor((L-1)/d)` removes `[1, L-1]`, leaving exactly `[L, R]`.

**Drill:** Multiples of 4 in [10, 30]? `floor(30/4) - floor(9/4) = 7 - 2 = 5` (12, 16, 20, 24, 28).

## 0.3 Digit count of n

**Fact:** Digit count = `Long.toString(n).length()` (most reliable for large longs).
Alternative: `(int) Math.log10(n) + 1` (precision issues for n near powers of 10).
Manual: `while (n > 0) { count++; n /= 10; }` (works but slower to write).

## 0.4 Integer overflow check (the reflex)

Before writing `a * b` where either could exceed ~46000, ask: "could this overflow int?"
- int max ≈ 2.1×10⁹
- Two ints near 10⁵ multiplied = 10¹⁰ → overflows
- Cast: `(long) a * b` ← cast happens BEFORE multiplication

## 0.5 Loop bound choice

When iterating from 1 to n:
- `for (int i = 1; i <= n; i++)` runs n times
- `for (int i = 0; i < n; i++)` runs n times
- `for (int i = 1; i < n; i++)` runs n-1 times (often a bug)

For sum 1+2+...+n use `1 <= i <= n`. For array of size n use `0 <= i < n`.

# Section 1 — Arithmetic sums (68 problems in this band)

## 1.1 Sum 1 + 2 + ... + n

**Fact:** `n(n+1)/2`.
**Derivation (so you OWN it):** pair the first with last (1+n), second with second-to-last (2+n-1), etc. Each pair = n+1. There are n/2 pairs. Total = `n(n+1)/2`.
**Cast:** for n = 10⁵, sum = ~5×10⁹ → overflows int.

**Drill:** Sum of 1..1000? `1000 × 1001 / 2 = 500,500`. Sum of 1..10⁵? `5×10⁹ + small change`.

**LC problems:**
- *Find N Unique Integers Sum up to Zero* (LC 1304, 1167)
- *Sum of Even Numbers After Queries* (LC 985, 1198)
- *Minimum Value to Get Positive Step by Step Sum* (LC 1413, 1212)

## 1.2 Sum of array — overflow check

**Fact:** Sum of n=10⁵ values each up to 10⁹ → up to 10¹⁴ → fits long, not int.

**LC problems:**
- *Running Sum of 1d Array* (LC 1480, 1104) — prefix sum baseline
- *Sum of Variable Length Subarrays* (LC 3427, 1215)
- *Matrix Diagonal Sum* (LC 1572, 1280)
- *Range Sum of BST* (LC 938, 1335)

## 1.3 Sum of digits

**Fact:** Loop `while (n > 0) { sum += n % 10; n /= 10; }`. Max digit sum of n ≤ 10⁹ ≈ 9 × 10 = 90 (cap matters).

**LC problems:**
- *Subtract the Product and Sum of Digits of an Integer* (LC 1281, 1141)
- *Sum of Digits in the Minimum Number* (LC 1085, 1256)
- *Sum of Digits of String After Convert* (LC 1945, 1254)
- *Calculate Digit Sum of a String* (LC 2243, 1301)

## 1.3b Arithmetic progression (AP) sum

**Added 2026-05-23 after verification — Calculate Money in LeetCode Bank (LC 1716, ~1394) was untagged but uses this.**

**Fact:** Sum of AP with first term `a`, common difference `d`, n terms:
- Last term: `a + (n-1)d`
- Sum: `n × (first + last) / 2`

**Drill:** Sum of 5, 8, 11, ..., 32 (10 terms)? Last = 5 + 9×3 = 32 ✓. Sum = 10 × (5+32)/2 = 185.

**Why it matters:** problems like "Hercy puts $X on day 1, $X+1 on day 2, ..." reduce to AP sum. Without the formula, you'd loop.

**LC problems:**
- *Calculate Money in LeetCode Bank* (LC 1716, ~1394)
- *Maximum Sum With Exactly K Elements* (LC 2656, 1213) — pick max k times, each +1 (AP)

## 1.4 Sum of squares

**Fact:** `1² + 2² + ... + n² = n(n+1)(2n+1)/6`.
**Less common at this band**, but appears in:
- *Sum of Squares of Special Elements* (LC 2778, 1151)
- *Subarrays Distinct Element Sum of Squares I* (LC 2913, 1297)

## 1.5 Prefix sum (technique, not formula — but math-adjacent)

**Fact:** `prefix[i] = prefix[i-1] + arr[i]`. Range sum `[l, r]` = `prefix[r] - prefix[l-1]`.
**Why this is a math reflex:** the off-by-one with `l-1` vs `l` is the same family as Section 0.1 range counting.

# Section 2 — Counting basics (52 problems)

## 2.1 Pair count (foundation)

**Fact:** Unordered pairs from n elements = `n(n-1)/2`.
**Drill:** Pairs from 5? `5×4/2 = 10`. From 10⁵? `≈ 5×10⁹` → overflow if you compute via int.

**LC problems:**
- *Count Number of Pairs With Absolute Difference K* (LC 2006, 1271)
- *Count Pairs That Form a Complete Day I* (LC 3184, 1149)
- *Count Pairs Whose Sum is Less than Target* (LC 2824, 1165)
- *Count Equal and Divisible Pairs in an Array* (LC 2176, 1215)

## 2.2 Pair count via frequency map (key pattern)

**Fact:** For each element x, find how many other elements y satisfy the relation. If relation depends only on equivalence class (mod, group, etc.), use frequency map: `for each y, pairs += freq[partner_of_y]`, then divide by 2 (or use one-pass with cumulative count).

**LC problems:** *Count Equal and Divisible Pairs in an Array* (LC 2176) — the canonical example. Same pattern as today's Caesar Cipher Pairs.

## 2.3 Running pair count (delta vs cumulative) — THE bug from today

**Fact:** At the k-th occurrence of an element, NEW pairs added = `k-1`. Total cumulative pairs in group = `C(k, 2) = k(k-1)/2`. **These are different. Don't mix.**

**Why this appears at this band:** several "count pairs as you iterate" problems use this incrementally.

## 2.4 Triple count

**Fact:** Triples from n = `n(n-1)(n-2)/6`. Same logic as pairs.
**LC problems:** *Count Good Triplets* (LC 1534, 1279), *Count Special Quadruplets* (LC 1995, 1352).

## 2.5 Counting via frequency map (general)

**LC problems:**
- *Count Elements With Maximum Frequency* (LC 3005, 1216)
- *Count Number of Distinct Integers After Reverse Operations* (LC 2442, 1218)
- *Count Common Words With One Occurrence* (LC 2085, 1307)

# Section 4 — Bit operations (40 problems)

## 4.1 Binary representation basics

**Facts:**
- Check bit i: `(n >> i) & 1`
- Set bit i: `n |= (1 << i)`
- Unset bit i: `n &= ~(1 << i)`
- Toggle bit i: `n ^= (1 << i)`

## 4.2 XOR identity

**Facts:** `a ^ a = 0`, `a ^ 0 = a`. XOR is commutative and associative.
**Why this matters at 1100+:** XOR is the trick for "find unique element when all others appear in pairs."

**LC problems:**
- *Convert Binary Number in a Linked List to Integer* (LC 1290, 1151)
- *Number of Steps to Reduce a Number in Binary Representation to One* (LC 1404, 1396)
- *Sum of All Subset XOR Totals* (LC 1863, 1372)
- *Sum of Values at Indices With K Set Bits* (LC 2859, 1218)

## 4.3 Count set bits

**Fact:** `Integer.bitCount(n)` returns popcount. Use this instead of writing loops.
**LC problems:** *Count Monobit Integers* (LC 3827, 1190), *Sum of Values at Indices With K Set Bits* (LC 2859).

## 4.4 Power of 2 anchor

**Fact:** `2^10 = 1024 ≈ 10³`. From this, `2^20 ≈ 10⁶`, `2^30 ≈ 10⁹`.
**Drill:** "n ≤ 20 means 2^n subsets = 10⁶ — brute enumeration OK."

# Section 6 — Permutation / combination (26 problems)

## 6.1 Permutation count of n distinct items

**Fact:** `n!`. For n=10, that's 3.6M — borderline brute force. For n=12, 479M — too slow. For n=20+, infeasible.
**Drill:** n=8 permutations? 40,320.

## 6.1b Constraint counting (stars-and-bars lite)

**Added 2026-05-23 after verification — Distribute Candies Among Children I (LC 2928, 1393) tests this.**

**Fact:** Count integer solutions to `x₁ + x₂ + ... + xₖ = n` with each `xᵢ` ≤ limit.

**At Band 1300:** brute force triple loop works because n ≤ 50 → 50³ = 125,000 ops. Just iterate `i` and `j`, derive `k = n - i - j`, check `0 ≤ k ≤ limit`. Count.

**At Band 1500+:** use stars and bars + inclusion-exclusion. Without bounds: solutions = `C(n+k-1, k-1)`. With bounds: subtract overcount via I-E.

**LC problems:**
- *Distribute Candies Among Children I* (LC 2928, 1393) — brute force OK
- *Distribute Candies Among Children II* (LC 3091, ~1700) — same problem with bigger constraints, needs the formula

## 6.2 nCr (informal at this band)

**Fact:** `n choose 2 = n(n-1)/2`, `n choose 3 = n(n-1)(n-2)/6`. Higher k uses the full formula `n!/(k!(n-k)!)` — appears more at Band 1500+.

**LC problems:**
- *Permutation Difference between Two Strings* (LC 3146, 1152)
- *Number of Ways to Buy Pens and Pencils* (LC 2240, 1399) — counting solutions of `a*x + b*y = total` (Diophantine flavor)

# Section 7 — Modular / divisibility (17 problems)

## 7.1 Mod basics

**Facts:**
- `a % b` returns the remainder. Java `%` preserves dividend sign: `-7 % 3 = -1`.
- `(a + b) % m = ((a % m) + (b % m)) % m`
- `(a * b) % m = ((a % m) * (b % m)) % m`
- Safe positive mod: `((a % m) + m) % m`

## 7.1b Parity reasoning (its own topic)

**Added 2026-05-23 after verification — Special Array I (LC 3151, 1152) and several other problems use parity as the central insight.**

**Fact:** A number is even iff `n & 1 == 0`. Equivalently `n % 2 == 0`. Faster reflex: use `&` for parity checks in tight loops.

**Patterns:**
- "Adjacent elements must have different parity" → just walk and check `(a ^ b) & 1`
- "Sum has fixed parity based on count of odd numbers" → count odd, check parity
- "Can you reach X by operations preserving parity?" → invariant check

**Why this is its own topic:** parity questions show up across many problem types (game theory, array constraints, sum properties). Recognizing parity as the underlying structure unlocks the answer faster.

**LC problems:**
- *Special Array I* (LC 3151, 1152) — adjacent parity check
- *Count Indices With Opposite Parity* (LC 3917, 1198)
- *Compute Alternating Sum* (LC 3701, 1228)

## 7.2 Divisibility test

**Fact:** `n % d == 0` iff d divides n.
**Common patterns at this band:**
- "Divisible by 3" — Fact: digit sum divisible by 3.
- "Divisible by 9" — Fact: digit sum divisible by 9.
- "Divisible by 11" — alternating digit sum divisible by 11.

**LC problems:**
- *Find Minimum Operations to Make All Elements Divisible by Three* (LC 3190, 1139)
- *Smallest Even Multiple* (LC 2413, 1144)
- *Sum Multiples* (LC 2652, 1182)
- *Divisible and Non-divisible Sums Difference* (LC 2894, 1140)
- *Minimum Operations to Make Array Sum Divisible by K* (LC 3512, 1228)

# Section 8 — Powers / squares / roots (13 problems)

## 8.1 Integer power

**Fact:** For small integer exponents, use direct multiplication (`i*i*i`) not `Math.pow(i, 3)`. `Math.pow` returns double — TLE in tight loops + precision issues.

## 8.2 Integer square / cube root

**Fact:** `(int) Math.sqrt(n)` can be off by 1 due to floating-point precision. Always verify: `int s = (int) Math.sqrt(n); while ((s+1)*(s+1) <= n) s++;`. Same for `Math.cbrt`.

**LC problems:**
- *Squares of a Sorted Array* (LC 977, 1129)
- *N-th Tribonacci Number* (LC 1137, 1142)

# Section 9b — Trick reasoning / one-line answers (added 2026-05-23 after verification)

Some Band 1200-1399 problems have a **one-line answer** that requires reasoning, not formula. The "math" is the insight, not the calculation.

**Pattern:** Read the problem, ask: "what's invariant across every operation?" The invariant gives you the formula directly.

## 9b.1 Tournament reduction

**Problem:** *Count of Matches in Tournament* (LC 1688, 1203).
**Insight:** Each match eliminates exactly 1 team. To go from n teams to 1 winner, you need n-1 eliminations. **Answer: n-1.** No simulation needed.
**Why this is math:** The reasoning IS the math. Once you see the invariant ("each match eliminates one team"), the algebra is trivial.

## 9b.2 General trick-problem heuristic

If a problem statement looks complex but constraints are small AND the answer feels like it should be one number:
1. Compute the answer for 2-3 small cases.
2. Look for a pattern.
3. Reason about WHY the pattern holds (find the invariant).

# Section 10b — Pigeonhole on states (introductory)

**Added 2026-05-23 after verification — Prison Cells After N Days (LC 957, 1392) was untagged by title regex but actually uses this pattern.**

## 10b.1 Pigeonhole on bounded state space

**Fact:** If a system has at most K distinct states and evolves deterministically, then within K+1 steps it MUST revisit a state — a cycle forms. After the cycle is detected, you can compute the answer at step N via modular arithmetic on the cycle length.

**Why this is Band 1300+ relevant:** at this band, brute-force simulation often works if you cap iterations and detect cycles. The pigeonhole reasoning is what gives you confidence to cap simulation at ~K iterations.

**Drill case — Prison Cells After N Days:** 8 cells, but the first and last are always 0 after day 1 (no neighbors). So effectively 6 cells = `2^6 = 64` distinct states. After at most 64 days, a state must repeat → cycle. Simulate ~14 days to find cycle, then `N % cycle_length` gives the answer.

**LC problems:**
- *Prison Cells After N Days* (LC 957, 1392)
- *Find the Winner of the Circular Game* (LC 1823, ~1500) — Josephus problem (Band 1500+ entry)

At Band 1700+, this becomes a CENTRAL skill — problems where no brute force exists and pigeonhole is the only way through.
